This city is in Russia. But to drive here from Moscow, you have to cross two other countries. So how did Russia end up with a city stuck between Poland and Lithuania?

Well, for seven hundred years, it wasn't Russian at all. In 1255, German crusaders called the Teutonic Knights built a castle here, and named it Königsberg. The King's Mountain.

It grew into the heart of Prussia. In 1701, its ruler crowned himself king right here. And its seven bridges were so famous, a mathematician turned them into a puzzle.

Then came 1945. The Red Army stormed Königsberg, and at Potsdam, Stalin asked for one prize: the north of East Prussia, with an ice-free port on the Baltic.

He got it. The city was renamed Kaliningrad. The Germans were sent away, and Russians moved in.

Back then, nobody minded. Lithuania was Soviet too, so you could drive straight home.

But in 1991, the Soviet Union collapsed. Lithuania walked away, and Kaliningrad was stranded.

Here's the twist. This had happened before. In 1919, with Germany's empire gone, a new strip of Poland cut this exact land off from Germany.

Two empires collapsed. And both times, the new border left this one city on the wrong side of the map.
